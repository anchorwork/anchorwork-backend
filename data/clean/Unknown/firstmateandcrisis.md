Crisis Communication & Suicide Prevention Best Practices Crisis Communication Methods (Digital vs. In-Person) Effective crisis communication relies on empathy, clarity, and calmness, whether it takes place via digital platforms (text/chat, phone, video) or in person. Key principles include active listening, a non-judgmental tone, and conveying genuine concern for the individual’s well-being . Below are best practices and adaptations for both digital and face-to-face crisis interactions: Core Techniques (All Settings): Remain calm, patient, and empathetic. Speak in a clear, soothing voice and use simple language. Avoid clinical jargon or robotic scripts – instead, use humanizing statements that show you care (e.g. “I’m here to help you and I’m listening”). Keep statements short and straightforward, and repeat important points gently if necessary . Always listen actively, allowing the person to express feelings without interruption . Acknowledge what they say by reflecting or validating (“It sounds like you’re feeling overwhelmed”) so they feel heard. Showing genuine concern helps build trust – crisis counselors are trained in “good contact” techniques to convey warmth and understanding, which has been shown to make interventions more effective . Digital Platform Communication: In text or online chats, where you lack tone of voice or body language, be extra mindful to explicitly express empathy in words. Use the person’s name and phrases that validate their feelings (“That sounds so painful. I’m so sorry you’re going through this.”). Since you cannot nod or show facial expressions, periodically summarize what they’ve said (“You mentioned feeling alone and hopeless…”) to confirm understanding. Ask open-ended questions to encourage them to share more. Typing style matters: use a warm and calm tone, avoid excessive punctuation or all caps (which can be misread), and clarify meanings if anything is ambiguous. On phone or video, focus on your tone – speak calmly and gently. Even via telehealth, you can build a strong therapeutic rapport; studies find little difference in empathy or attentiveness between phone and in-person sessions when done thoughtfully . The key is to continuously engage with compassion and stay present with the person’s words (e.g. brief verbal affirmations like “mm-hmm, I hear you”). On digital platforms, also pace the conversation by checking in if the person goes silent (“I’m still here with you; take your time”). This assures them you haven’t left and encourages continued dialogue. In-Person Communication: In face-to-face crises, non-verbal communication is crucial. Maintain a calm, open posture: keep your hands visible and uncrossed, and give the person physical space so they don’t feel trapped . If safe to do so, you might sit at the same level as them (avoiding a dominating stance) and nod or use concerned facial expressions to show you’re attuned. Avoid any behavior that could be perceived as threatening – for example, do not block exits and be mindful not to loom over or crowd them . Eye contact is useful to show attentiveness, but don’t stare unblinkingly; if the individual is agitated or paranoid, soft eye contact or briefly looking away can reduce perceived pressure. Use a gentle tone even if the person raises their voice . It’s important to depersonalize any anger or profanity directed at you – stay composed and do not argue or yell back . Your calm presence can help de-escalate the situation. Additionally, offer small comforts when appropriate: a glass of water, a blanket, or a quieter space can show care. In person you can also leverage touch carefully – only if appropriate and with permission (for example, a light touch on the shoulder or hand to convey support) – but be cautious, as not everyone will be receptive to touch in crisis. Overall, whether digitally or in-person, the communication style should be human-centered, empathetic, and authentic, never cold or overly scripted. The individual in crisis should feel with someone, not talking to a distant robot. Suicide Prevention Protocols (WHO, Lifeline, SAMHSA) Global and national organizations have established suicide prevention protocols and guidelines to ensure safe, effective care for individuals at risk. These protocols cover everything from broad prevention strategies to specific steps crisis counselors should follow. Integrating these best practices into Anchorwork’s emergency response system will ensure interventions align with proven standards. World Health Organization (Global Best Practices): The WHO emphasizes that suicide prevention requires a comprehensive, multi-pronged approach. Its evidence-based framework “LIVE LIFE” recommends four key interventions every national strategy should include: restrict access to means of suicide, ensure responsible media reporting, foster socio-emotional life skills (particularly in youth), and early identify and support anyone showing suicidal behavior . For example, reducing access to lethal means (like firearms or pesticides) has been proven to save lives, and teaching coping and problem-solving skills to adolescents can build resilience . WHO also underscores cross-cutting pillars such as multi-sector collaboration, public awareness, and healthcare provider training . In practice, this means communities, healthcare systems, schools, and media outlets should work together to spot warning signs, intervene early, and connect people to help. The WHO advises health workers worldwide to ask about suicidal thoughts when warning signs are present, to listen without judgment, and to ensure at-risk individuals are not left alone and receive follow-up care. Culturally sensitive care is important as well, since suicide risk can be influenced by local factors. In summary, global best practices call for proactive identification of those at risk and a coordinated response to keep individuals safe, with the ultimate goal of zero suicides as an aspirational target. U.S. National Protocols – 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline: In the United States, the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (formerly National Suicide Prevention Lifeline) provides 24/7 phone, text, and chat support via a network of crisis centers. The Lifeline has standard protocols to ensure consistency and safety. All callers are directly asked about suicidal ideation – it is policy to gently but frankly inquire, “Are you thinking about suicide?” as a routine part of every crisis contact . If the person answers affirmatively or there’s any indication of risk, the counselor then conducts a full suicide risk assessment covering the intensity of ideation, any plan or prior attempts, and the person’s available support . This assessment guides the intervention. Lifeline counselors use an approach called “active engagement”: rather than immediately dispatching emergency services, they first collaboratively work with the individual to reduce risk. This may involve creating a safety plan together (identifying coping strategies the person can use, reasons to keep living, and who they can contact for support) and asking if they will agree to safe themselves (committing to not act on suicidal urges for a defined period) . High engagement and empathy are critical – research shows that when counselors establish trust and rapport (showing genuine care and understanding), it often de-escalates the imminent danger and avoids the need to involve police or ambulances . However, if a caller is in immediate danger (imminent risk) – for instance, they have taken steps to end their life or are about to – the Lifeline’s protocol is to initiate an “active rescue.” This means the counselor gathers information about the person’s location and works to send emergency services as a last resort to ensure safety. (Where possible, this is done with the person’s knowledge and collaboration, to maintain trust.) In December 2022, the Lifeline introduced a unified Suicide Safety Policy that standardized these practices across all centers, replacing older guidelines on risk assessment and imminent risk intervention . The policy ensures every center follows the same core steps: assess risk level uniformly, use the least invasive intervention necessary, and involve emergency responders only when absolutely necessary to save a life . Additionally, the Lifeline encourages follow-up contact for people at high risk – many centers will call or text back a person a day or two after an acute crisis to check in, which has been shown to reduce subsequent suicide attempts. Overall, the U.S. Lifeline protocol centers on compassionate, person-centered engagement, thorough risk evaluation, safety planning, and emergency action when needed, aligning with the goal of suicide safer care. SAMHSA & National Guidelines for Crisis Care: The Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA) in the U.S. has published national guidelines for behavioral health crisis services (most recently updated in 2020 and 2025) to shape how communities respond to mental health crises, including suicidal crises. These guidelines present an ideal model of a “no wrong door” crisis care system. Key elements include: regional 24/7 crisis call centers (like 988) that can triage and support callers, centrally-dispatched mobile crisis teams that can meet people in person where they are, and crisis receiving/stabilization facilities that operate 24/7 for anyone who needs a safe place for observation and care . By having someone to talk to, someone to respond in person, and somewhere to go, individuals in crisis are never turned away . In terms of protocol, SAMHSA’s best-practice toolkit emphasizes several essential principles for crisis intervention: it should be recovery-oriented (helping the person stabilize and find hope), trauma-informed (sensitive to past trauma and avoiding re-traumatization), and utilize peer support whenever possible . Peer counselors (people with lived experience of recovery) play a significant role in many U.S. crisis programs, as they can connect with callers on a human level and provide hope through their example . Another pillar is the Zero Suicide initiative (sometimes called “suicide safer care”), which SAMHSA endorses for health systems: it means making suicide prevention a core responsibility of care, including systematic screening for suicide risk in all clinical encounters, developing suicide-specific care plans, reducing access to lethal means, and continuous follow-up for those at risk . All staff are trained in these interventions so that no plea for help falls through the cracks. In practice, SAMHSA’s guidelines translate to protocols like: do not use force or law enforcement unless absolutely necessary, engage the person in decision-making about their care, address basic needs and stressors, and connect them to ongoing support once the immediate crisis passes. By integrating these standards, an emergency response system ensures it is aligned with both global ideals and U.S. best practices – providing care that is accessible, compassionate, and effective in reducing suicide risk. Signs of Acute Suicide Risk (Verbal, Behavioral, & Physical Indicators) Recognizing the warning signs of suicide is critical for timely intervention. These signs can be verbal (what a person says or writes), behavioral (what they do), emotional/psychological (what they feel or express), and even physiological. Below are specific signs in each category that indicate heightened suicide risk: Verbal Signs: Often, people considering suicide will express feelings of hopelessness or burden in conversation. Pay attention to any statement that indicates despair, self-hate, or a wish to die. Examples include expressions like “I have no reason to live,” “No sense of purpose in life,” or “People would be better off without me” . They might also talk about feeling trapped or unbearable pain: e.g. “There’s no way out,” “I can’t take this anymore.” Some may directly mention death or suicide – any talk of wanting to die, wanting to kill oneself, or planning suicide is an urgent red flag. Even indirect hints (like “Soon you won’t have to worry about me”) should be taken seriously. Written posts or text messages conveying these sentiments count as verbal signs too. Suicidal threats, whether spoken (“I’m going to end it all”) or written, are an obvious danger sign and should prompt immediate action. Always err on the side of caution: if someone is talking about death or demonstrating a preoccupation with dying, assume they might be at risk and engage with care and concern. Behavioral Signs: Changes in behavior, especially actions that signal preparation for death or disconnection from life, can indicate acute risk. Watch for withdrawal from friends, family, and usual activities – e.g., the person suddenly stops attending work or school, avoids social interaction, or isolates themselves in their room for long periods. Conversely, some individuals may exhibit reckless or self-destructive behaviors: driving dangerously, increasing alcohol or drug use, or otherwise taking unusual risks as if they have little to lose . A surge in substance abuse can be a major warning sign, as it impairs judgment and can deepen depression. Another common sign is putting affairs in order or giving away prized possessions unexpectedly . For instance, they might settle debts, draft a will, or hand off personal items they always cared about – these can be signals of suicidal planning, as the person is preparing for the possibility of not being around. Even subtle actions like saying goodbye or thank you in a way that feels final, or suddenly visiting people they care about out-of-the-blue (as if to pay last respects), should raise concern. Additionally, if someone has been agitated or enraged, pay attention if they start acting very calm or resigned all of a sudden – that could indicate they feel “resolved” by deciding on suicide (this is a paradoxical warning sign: a previously anxious person becoming eerily calm can mean they believe they’ve found a “solution,” i.e., suicide). Any dramatic change in behavior or routines for the worse – such as neglecting personal hygiene, or, on the flip side, an inexplicable uplift in mood after a period of depression (which could mean they have set a plan) – should be carefully and gently probed. Emotional/Psychological Signs: Certain mood states and expressions of emotion are closely linked with suicide risk. Profound hopelessness is a hallmark – the person sees no hope for the future or feels “dead inside.” They might say things like “What’s the point? It will never get better.” Look for sustained sadness or depression, as well as loss of interest in activities they used to enjoy. Intense anxiety or agitation can also elevate risk; the person may appear extremely on edge, restless, or panicky. They might be unable to sleep (insomnia) or conversely, sleep all the time as an escape . Extreme mood swings are another sign – for example, crying spells or bursts of anger out of proportion to the situation . Rage or uncontrolled anger, or seeking revenge for perceived wrongs, has been noted in some suicidal individuals (especially if combined with feelings of being trapped) . In people with certain conditions, psychotic symptoms could appear or worsen – for instance, someone with schizophrenia might experience command hallucinations telling them to kill themselves , which is obviously high risk. Pay attention to increased talk of feeling unbearable guilt, shame, or being a burden – these emotions often underlie suicidal thoughts. Despair and social disconnection (“no one cares about me”) are psychologically heavy warning signs. If you observe a person’s affect to be flat (emotionally numb) or conversely highly desperate, take note. Often it’s this psychological pain – feelings of entrapment, burdensomeness, and hopelessness – that drives suicidal ideation . Recognizing these emotional signals allows a responder to intervene with empathy (“It sounds like you’re feeling completely hopeless… I understand why that’s overwhelming. Let’s talk about what’s causing those feelings.”). Physical/Biometric Signs: In some cases, you may notice physiological indicators of extreme distress which, while not specific to suicide, signal that the person is in an acute emotional crisis. The body often mirrors the mind’s stress. For example, someone highly anxious or panicked may display a racing heart, rapid breathing or hyperventilation, sweating, or trembling hands . They might report feeling chills or nausea, chest pain or a tightness in the chest, or even complain of physical pain with no clear cause – all possible signs of severe anxiety or panic attacks . A person on the verge of a suicide attempt might appear notably agitated and unable to sit still, pacing the room, or, conversely, they might seem unusually lethargic, exhausted, and slow-moving (sometimes a side effect of depression or medication overdose). Tearfulness is common, but some individuals might also show a startling lack of reactivity (in shock or dissociating). If you have access to biometric data (as some modern crisis systems do via wearables or apps), watch for spikes in heart rate, blood pressure, or indicators like reduced heart rate variability which can accompany acute stress. Facial expressions can also be telling – a face contorted in grief, or a hollow, distant look. While these physical signs are supportive evidence more than definitive proof of suicidal intent, they help paint a fuller picture of the person’s state. In essence, any sudden or extreme change in a person’s physiological or behavioral presentation, combined with psychological despair, should be treated as a potential emergency. Trust your gut: if it looks like someone is not okay, step in and ask – it could save a life. (Remember: The presence of one or more of these signs warrants a direct, compassionate conversation about suicide. Asking “Are you thinking about hurting yourself or ending your life?” will not plant the idea in their head – instead, it can bring relief and open the door to getting help. In an emergency, do not leave a suicidal person alone and contact professional help immediately.) De-Escalation Techniques in Crisis Intervention De-escalation techniques are tools and approaches used to calm an intensely emotional or agitated situation, helping the person in crisis regain composure and feel safer. In the context of suicide prevention or any mental health crisis, effective de-escalation can mean the difference between a conversation that leads someone back from the brink versus a situation that spirals out of control. These techniques are focused on reducing anxiety, lowering tension, and building cooperation between the person in crisis and the responder. Here are evidence-based de-escalation practices (often taught in crisis intervention training) that Anchorwork’s responders can employ: Stay Calm and Center Yourself: Your demeanor sets the tone. Take a slow breath before you engage. Maintain a steady, relaxed tone of voice and a composed body language . If you appear anxious, angry, or hurried, it can fuel the person’s own panic or aggression. Do not take anything said personally – people in crisis might yell, curse, or direct frustration at you; respond with patience and do not retaliate or raise your voice . By keeping your cool, you demonstrate control of the situation and create a stable atmosphere for the person to begin calming down. Respect Personal Space and Safety: Ensure the physical environment feels non-threatening. Maintain an appropriate distance (roughly two arm’s lengths is often suggested) so the person doesn’t feel cornered . If in person, stand at an angle instead of directly face-to-face, which can feel confrontational. Do not block doorways or exits – the person should not feel physically trapped . If you’re with someone actively agitated, you might say “I’m going to take a couple steps back just so you feel more comfortable, okay.” Also, avoid sudden movements. Keep your hands where they can see them (such as loosely at your sides or in a relaxed open posture) . This all signals that you are not a threat. In a digital context, “personal space” translates to not overwhelming the person with messages – give them time to respond and don’t bombard with questions if they disengage momentarily. Use a Soft, Concise Communication Style: When someone is in crisis, their ability to process information is impaired by intense emotions. Speak in short, simple sentences and use a gentle, reassuring voice . For example, instead of a long complex explanation, give one instruction or thought at a time: “Let’s take a breath together.” Pause between sentences so they have a chance to absorb your words. It’s often effective to repeat key points calmly (broken-record technique) if the person isn’t registering them at first . Repetition (without raising volume) can help cut through the mental chaos. Keep your language non-technical and supportive. Avoid issuing rapid-fire questions or ultimatums. The goal is to be heard as a helper, not as another source of stress. Listen and Validate: One of the most powerful de-escalation tools is simply listening – genuinely, deeply, and non-judgmentally. Allow the individual to vent if they need to. Often, a person in crisis may be yelling or crying about a situation; let them express it. Acknowledge their feelings – you can say things like “I hear you. This is really hard – I can tell you’re feeling enraged and hurt.” Show that you are truly paying attention: nod (if in person), use verbal affirmations (“uh-huh,” “I understand”) and echo back what they say occasionally (“You feel like no one ever listens to you, is that right?”). Validating does not mean you necessarily agree with any delusions or harmful talk; it means you accept that their feelings are real and important. For example, “I understand you feel everyone is against you – that must be a very painful feeling.” This can help the person feel less alone in their experience and often you will notice their intensity start to lower when they feel understood . Do not criticize or argue about their point of view – avoid statements that dismiss or minimize what they’re going through. Even if their thoughts are irrational, now is not the time to correct them; first you must get them calm and engaged with you. Find Common Ground & Avoid Power Struggles: If the person in crisis is upset about something or expressing hostile viewpoints, don’t directly confront or contradict every statement. Look for points of agreement or at least understanding. For instance, if they say “No one cares about me,” instead of arguing “That’s not true, plenty of people care,” you might respond, “It certainly feels like you’re completely alone right now.” You are aligning with their feelings, which are undeniable, rather than arguing about facts. Agree where you can (“Yes, it’s unfair you were treated that way”) or agree to disagree calmly if needed . The key is to avoid saying “you’re wrong” outright or doing anything that the person might perceive as an attack, which will only escalate defensiveness. If they direct anger at you, you might say, “I want to help and not fight. Let’s work on this together.” By collaborating rather than commanding, you reduce the “us vs. them” dynamic. Never belittle their demands or concerns, even if they seem irrational. Stay away from making threats or dares (e.g. never say “Calm down, or I can’t help you” or worse, “Go ahead then!”). Instead, focus on solutions and “we”: “Let’s figure this out,” “We will get through this moment, I promise.” Set Gentle Limits and Offer Choices: While avoiding power struggles, it’s still important to maintain boundaries for safety if the person’s behavior is dangerous or highly inappropriate. Setting limits in a crisis means clearly and calmly stating what is acceptable and what isn’t, without issuing harsh ultimatums. For example, if a person starts to throw objects or wants to run off, you might say, “I understand you’re angry, but I need you to not throw that, okay? I don’t want anyone to get hurt. You can scream if you need to, but no throwing.” The tone here is firm but caring – you’re not yelling orders, you’re explaining boundaries and the consequences in a non-threatening way . Another effective tactic is to give the person small choices to restore their sense of control: “Would you like to continue talking here, or maybe we can step outside for some fresh air? It’s up to you.” Offering two safe options empowers them and can reduce agitation by avoiding feelings of entrapment. “Choice” language can also be used like: “You can keep talking to me here, or if you prefer, I can call someone you trust to be with you. What would you like?” By giving options, you show respect for their autonomy. Just ensure that both choices are safe ones you can support. Setting limits is about creating a structured calm – not to boss the person around, but to gently steer them away from harm. Use Supportive, Reassuring Statements: Throughout the interaction, reassure the person of their safety and your intention to help. People in crisis are often terrified – of themselves, of others, of consequences. Simple, grounding reassurances can help: say “You are safe here with me right now”, “I’m here to help you, not to hurt you” , “I promise I will stay with you through this”. If appropriate, lower yourself to their eye level or sit down, which is less intimidating, and say, “Let’s take this one step at a time.” Let them know they’re not alone: “I’m right here with you. We’ll get through this together.” Such statements, when sincere, can gradually diffuse fear and anger. Be honest and transparent as well – if you are calling for additional help or need to take an action, explain what you’re doing so they don’t feel betrayed. For example, “I’m going to call my teammate, who knows a lot about situations like this, to get some ideas. I want to make sure we handle this the best way for you.” This keeps trust intact. Body Language and Presence: In-person, your body language can either escalate or de-escalate. Adopt a neutral, attentive posture – for instance, uncross your arms, keep an open stance, and relax your face . A calm facial expression (with concern and empathy) signals that you are not upset or afraid – this can be calming in itself. Avoid pointing or sudden gestures; slow, deliberate movements are better. If the person is seated and you’re standing, consider sitting down to appear less authoritative (but only if you feel safe to do so). Allow the person to pace or fidget if they need to; don’t demand they sit still, as that can increase their feeling of being controlled. If you’re on a phone call, you obviously rely on voice tone alone – so you might nod or smile to put yourself in a friendly mindset (even though they can’t see it, it can affect the warmth of your voice). Match their energy level carefully: not by yelling if they yell, but by showing urgency in a calm way if they are frantic (e.g., “I hear how intense this is for you – I’m paying full attention, let’s work on it together.”). This shows you’re taking them seriously. Never touch the person during a heated moment unless you ask and they explicitly consent – unwanted touch can be misinterpreted as aggression or violation of space. However, if you have a good rapport or it’s a loved one, sometimes a light touch or holding their hand (with permission) can be very grounding. Use your judgment and always prioritize their comfort, not your own desire to console. After De-escalation, Transition to Problem-Solving: As the person begins to calm down (you may notice their breathing slowing, voice softening, or posture relaxing), continue to maintain the supportive stance. Once stability is regained, you can gently move toward the next steps: for instance, collaboratively develop a plan (“Now that you’re feeling a little calmer, maybe we can talk about what will help going forward. Would you like to call your sister to come be with you tonight?”). The end phase of de-escalation is to reinforce any positive coping the person did (“Thank you for trusting me and talking to me about this”). If appropriate, offer praise for small gains: “I know this was really hard – you did a great job taking those breaths to calm down.” This can boost their confidence that they can handle the situation. Ensure that resources or next steps are in place (like making sure they are not alone, or connecting them with professional help immediately if needed). De-escalation isn’t a solution by itself, but a bridge to get the person to accept further help once they’re in a more rational state of mind. In summary, de-escalation in a crisis is about being that steady, empathetic presence that can absorb the person’s fear or anger without mirror­ing it, and gradually guiding them back to a state of relative calm. It requires patience, emotional regulation on the part of the responder, and a toolbox of these verbal and non-verbal skills practiced over time. By integrating these techniques, Anchorwork responders can navigate high-tension moments with confidence and compassion, anchoring the person in distress to a sense of safety and hope. Emotional Re-Anchoring and Grounding Strategies When someone is overwhelmed by intense emotions, panic, or trauma, grounding and emotional re-anchoring techniques can help bring them back to the present moment and restore a sense of stability. Grounding refers to a variety of practices that help a person “reconnect” to the here-and-now – literally to ground them in reality away from distressing thoughts or memories. Emotional re-anchoring is a similar concept: it means helping someone shift out of a chaotic emotional state and anchor themselves in a calmer, safer state (often by using familiar or soothing stimuli). These techniques are extremely useful in crisis intervention once the individual is de-escalated enough to participate, as well as for the individuals themselves to regain control. Here are several proven grounding and re-anchoring strategies: Sensory Grounding (5-4-3-2-1 Technique): One of the most common grounding exercises is to engage the five senses to orient a person to the present. Encourage the individual to describe their immediate environment in detail, which forces the mind away from internal turmoil. For example, have them name 5 things they see, 4 things they can feel (touch), 3 things they hear, 2 things they smell, and 1 thing they taste. You can do a simpler variation if needed: “Look around and tell me what you see. Now listen – what sounds can you hear in the room? Okay, can you touch the chair you’re sitting on and describe its texture?” By systematically focusing on external sensory input, the person’s racing thoughts may slow down. A sample approach might be: “Take a moment to notice what’s around you right now – what color are the walls? Is there a rug under your feet? Can you feel it?” This technique helps bring the person out of their head and into the real, safe space they are currently in . You might say, for instance: “You’re here in this room with me now, it’s Tuesday afternoon, the window is letting in some light – can you feel the chair under you? Good. What else do you notice around us?” Such guidance, as simple as it sounds, can dramatically reduce dissociation and panic . Breathing Exercises and Muscle Relaxation: Controlled breathing is a fundamental grounding tool because it directly calms the physiological stress response. Guide the person through slow, deep breaths. A common method is the 4-7-8 breathing or just a simple slow inhale-exhale cycle: for example, “Breathe in deeply through your nose for 4 seconds… now exhale slowly through your mouth for 6 seconds. Let’s do it together a few times.” You can also instruct them to place a hand on their belly and watch/feel it rise and fall with each breath, which reinforces focus on the body . Deep breathing counteracts the hyperventilation of anxiety and can reduce a pounding heart within minutes. Pairing breathing with muscle relaxation enhances grounding: you might do a quick progressive muscle relaxation, asking them to clench their fists tightly for 5 seconds and then release , noticing the difference between tension and relaxation. Similarly, have them shrug their shoulders up to their ears, hold, then drop them down – physically releasing tension that often accumulates during crisis. These actions not only distract from emotional pain but also send signals of safety to the brain. Emphasize breathing throughout: “Keep taking slow breaths… good. Feel the air filling your lungs… now breathe out any stress.” Sometimes even just one deep sigh-like exhale can shift the nervous system towards calm. “Here and Now” Orientation: An important aspect of re-anchoring is to remind the person that in this moment, they are safe (assuming that’s true). Gently remind them of where they are and that the threatening memory or scenario is not happening right now. For instance, someone having a flashback or severe anxiety might feel like a past trauma is real again. You can say: “I know your mind is replaying that scary event. Let’s look around: you’re here in [this place], and it’s [date/time]. That event is not happening now. You made it through; you are safe today.” Encourage them to state some basic facts out loud: “Can you tell me your name? What day of the week is it? Who is in the room with you?” This might seem simplistic, but these questions can pull someone out of a disorienting mental loop and ground them in current reality . Another tactic is to ask about near-future events: “What’s something you plan to do tomorrow? Maybe something small like having your morning coffee – can you describe how you like it?” Shifting focus to later today or tomorrow helps move their mindset past the immediate emotional storm . If the person is dissociating (feeling unreal or detached), you can also guide them to tap their feet on the floor or touch a nearby object and describe it, which helps reconnect mind and body . Throughout, use the person’s name and reassure them frequently: “, stay with me here. You’re doing great. Keep focusing on right now.” Visual Imagery and Mental Anchoring: Our imagination can be a powerful ally in grounding. Guided imagery involves having the person visualize something comforting or neutral to shift their emotional state. One common exercise is to guide them to their “safe place.” In a calm voice, ask them to think of a place (real or imagined) where they feel safe, relaxed, or happy – it could be a beach, a cozy room, a favorite childhood spot. Then prompt them to describe it: “You’re at the beach – imagine the warm sand under your feet, the sound of gentle waves, the salty smell of the air… What do you see? Are there seagulls? Good… Just breathe and be there for a moment.” This can provide an emotional refuge that lowers distress. Another technique is the “emotional dial” or volume knob visualization : if they rate their anxiety as a 9/10, have them imagine a dial in their mind and turn it down slowly – see if they can “dial it down” to an 8, then 7, guiding them with each breath. Some may find it helpful to visualize their negative thoughts as a loud TV or radio, and then mentally lower the volume or change the channel . This externalization of the thought stream can reduce its power. Imagery of containment can also help: suggest they picture boxing up their intense emotions or stress and setting the box aside for the moment – they can “pick it up later” when they’re ready, but for now it’s sealed. Not every person can easily do visualization under stress, but with practice and gentle coaching it can be effective. The key is to find a mental image that resonates as calming or empowering for them (some might prefer imagining themselves as strong or remembering a moment they felt proud or loved). Engage in Small Grounding Tasks: Doing a simple, concrete task can ground a person by focusing their mind. You might ask the individual to count something in the room (ceiling tiles, tiles on the floor, books on a shelf) or do a little mental exercise like saying the alphabet backwards or counting down from 100 by sevens. These tasks are cognitively engaging enough to divert the mind from emotional pain, but not so hard as to cause more frustration. Another approach is drawing or writing: give them a pen and paper and encourage them to scribble, draw a symbol of what they’re feeling, or write down what’s going through their head in a stream-of-consciousness. The act of writing can be cathartic and organizing. If the person is too overwhelmed to do that, even holding something cold or warm can help – e.g., have them hold an ice cube or a warm cup of tea and focus on that sensation (extreme temperatures can snap one’s attention to the present). Grounding objects are useful too: some crisis counselors carry things like a smooth stone, a stress ball, or a soft piece of fabric to hand to someone in distress and ask them to describe the texture. This tactile focus can be very soothing. Similarly, asking the person to stand up and do a stretch or small exercise (if they have been frozen in panic) can break the freeze response. For instance, “Can you stand up with me and stretch your arms to the sky? Okay, now shake out your hands.” Movement anchors them back into their body. Soothing and Re-Anchoring Techniques: These are meant to evoke positive feelings or comfort. Encourage self-soothing actions that the person finds comforting. If they have a pet or a stuffed animal, suggest they cuddle with it (physical contact with pets has a well-known calming effect, lowering blood pressure and anxiety) . If a loved one is present or on standby, facilitate contact or a gentle hug (with consent). You can also guide the person to give themselves compassion: a technique from DBT/CBT is to have them repeat a kind phrase to themselves – for example: “You’re going to be okay. This is really hard, but you’re doing your best.” They can say this internally or out loud. Repeating such affirmations can gradually shift their inner dialogue from panic to reassurance. Another approach is to have them recall a positive memory or the face of someone they love . Ask: “Can you think of someone who makes you feel safe or cared about? Maybe your grandmother, or a close friend. Picture them here with you, telling you that you will get through this.” This visualization can re-anchor them in feelings of love and support. Some individuals find it soothing to pray or invoke spiritual affirmations if that’s in line with their beliefs – you can encourage this if appropriate (e.g., “If you have a prayer or mantra that gives you strength, now is a good time to say it.”). Music can also be grounding: playing a calming song the person likes, or even humming a simple tune together, can regulate emotions. Essentially, any healthy sensory or emotional stimulus that brings comfort, familiarity, or hope can serve as an anchor. The trick is to guide the person to it when they are too overwhelmed to remember to do it themselves. Overall, grounding techniques “anchor” a person’s awareness to the present and to their own resilience. In a storm of crisis, these are like the anchor that keeps the ship from drifting further into danger. It’s important to personalize grounding strategies – different individuals will respond to different techniques, so having a variety in your toolkit (sensory, cognitive, imaginative, physical) is valuable. Encourage the person to practice some of these when they are not in crisis too, so they become familiar coping skills. In an acute moment, calmly lead them through one or two strategies that seem best suited, and observe – you will often see the person start to breathe slower, gain color in their face, or reduce crying as they re-center. Once grounded, the individual is more capable of engaging in constructive steps like creating a safety plan or accepting further help. Human connection itself is a powerful anchor, so throughout all these techniques, continue to accompany the person with a steady, compassionate presence: this combination of grounding and empathy can truly guide someone from the edge of despair back to a place of stability and hope.
